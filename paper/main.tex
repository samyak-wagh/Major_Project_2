\begin{abstract}
General-purpose chatbots answer operating-systems (OS) questions fluently but not necessarily from the course textbook. We present OS~Tutor, a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) tutor over a standard OS textbook that runs with either a locally hosted, LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct model or a cloud-hosted open-weight model. We contribute a leakage-controlled evaluation at two levels. At the system level, six configurations (with and without retrieval, with the cloud model, the fine-tuned model and the un-tuned base model) are compared on 17 questions screened for overlap with the fine-tuning data, with bootstrap intervals and a judge-free numerical benchmark of 27 problems. At the generator level, the adapter is compared with its base on 97 held-out items over three seeds. RAG with the cloud model is the most faithful system (0.61 versus 0.32 for RAG with the fine-tuned model). Fine-tuning improves token-F1 on held-out passages from 0.321 to 0.393 (95\% CI of the difference 0.039 to 0.104) but not RAGAS answer relevancy, and its effect depends on the system prompt. A data audit shows why: 762 examples rest on 126 passages, refusal is taught by 14 distinct questions, and about half of the recall answers lie outside their passages. The study indicates that, at this data scale, fine-tuning a small model mainly changes answer style, and that data grounding and prompt consistency matter more than model size.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Students increasingly study from general-purpose chatbots, whose fluent answers are not tied to their course material and may contain errors a novice cannot detect~\cite{kasneci2023edu,ji2023hallucination}. We therefore developed a specialised chatbot for specific coursework, grounded in the standard textbook of the course. We chose Operating Systems (OS) as the first course; our aim is to extend the same approach to further courses, each built on its own standard textbooks. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis2020rag} supplies retrieved textbook passages to the model, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning such as LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} and QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} makes it possible to adapt a small model on modest hardware, so that a tutor can run locally and offline.

We ask two questions. \textbf{RQ1:} how does a small, local, fine-tuned model with RAG compare with the same pipeline using a larger cloud model? \textbf{RQ2:} what does fine-tuning add beyond the un-tuned base model, in grounded answering, refusal and calculation? RQ2 can only be answered with an evaluation free of train/test overlap, and our own data audit showed how easily overlap arises.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{System.} OS~Tutor, an end-to-end RAG tutor with interchangeable local and cloud generators sharing one retriever (Section~\ref{sec:system}).
  \item \textbf{Data audit.} A 762-example instruction dataset in seven categories, and an audit that quantifies passage reuse, refusal diversity and answer groundedness (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item \textbf{System-level study.} Six systems on 17 overlap-screened questions with a base-model control and paired bootstrap intervals, plus a judge-free numerical benchmark (Sections~\ref{sec:eval} and~\ref{sec:resA}).
  \item \textbf{Generator-level study.} A passage-grouped, three-seed comparison of adapter and base on 97 held-out items under two system prompts (Section~\ref{sec:resB}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{RAG and fine-tuning.} Lewis et al.~\cite{lewis2020rag} pair a generator with a retrieval index, and dense retrievers~\cite{karpukhin2020dpr} made passage retrieval practical; we use a simple dense baseline. LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} and QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} train low-rank updates on a frozen, optionally 4-bit base; we apply them to a 1.5B model of the Qwen2.5 family~\cite{qwen25}. Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2023lima} argue that a small curated instruction set mainly teaches style and format, consistent with our findings.

\textbf{Abstention.} Models must learn to recognise unanswerable questions~\cite{rajpurkar2018squad2}, which matters in education, where confident errors are costly~\cite{kasneci2023edu}. Our \texttt{OUT\_OF\_CONTEXT} examples are a simple form of abstention training.

\textbf{Evaluation.} RAGAS~\cite{es2024ragas} defines LLM-judged faithfulness, answer relevancy and context-precision metrics; LLM judges have known biases~\cite{zheng2023judge}, so we add reference-based metrics such as ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge} and a judge-free benchmark. Contamination between training and evaluation data inflates results~\cite{magar2022contamination}; we screen for it and test differences with the paired bootstrap~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}.

\section{System Description}\label{sec:system}
\textbf{Retrieval.} The knowledge base is one OS textbook~\cite{galvin}, parsed with PyMuPDF and split into 1000-character chunks with 200-character overlap (4{,}330 chunks). Chunks are embedded with BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5~\cite{xiao2023cpack} and stored in Qdrant; at query time the top three chunks above a similarity threshold of 0.55 are retrieved. All RAG systems share this retriever.

\textbf{Generators.} The local generator is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with our LoRA adapter on CPU in 32-bit precision, decoded with sampling (temperature 0.1, top-$p$ 0.85, repetition penalty 1.1, at most 256 new tokens), with passages truncated to 300 words. The cloud generator is \texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b}~\cite{gptoss} served through Groq. The un-tuned base model is obtained from the same weights by bypassing the adapter. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline; in no-RAG mode the retrieval branch is skipped.

\textbf{Two system prompts.} The \emph{training prompt}, used for every fine-tuning example, enforces strict grounding: answer only from the passages, otherwise reply \texttt{OUT\_OF\_CONTEXT}. The \emph{deployed prompt} used by the serving code asks for a concise tutor answer of two to five sentences, based on the passages if provided, with no refusal instruction. The system-level study runs under the deployed prompt; the generator-level study tests both.

\section{Fine-Tuning Data and Method}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Dataset and Audit}\label{sec:audit}
The dataset has 762 chat-format examples, each a system message, a user message of the form \texttt{Textbook passages: <passage> Question: <question>}, and an answer. \emph{Recall} examples ask for one fact from a curated knowledge-base entry; \emph{applied\_numerical} problems have worked answers produced by simulating the algorithm; four reasoning categories were authored individually and are scarce; and \emph{out\_of\_context} examples pair a real OS passage with an unrelated question, the answer being \texttt{OUT\_OF\_CONTEXT}.

Table~\ref{tab:audit} audits the data. \emph{Ungrounded} means that fewer than 30\% of the answer's content words occur in its passage, a cheap lexical proxy. Four findings shape the interpretation of every result below. (i)~The 762 examples use only \textbf{126 distinct passages}. (ii)~In the original split, \textbf{58 of 60 validation examples shared a passage with training}, so validation loss partly measures memorisation. (iii)~The 40 refusal examples use \textbf{14 distinct questions}. (iv)~\textbf{46\% of recall answers} (133 of 287) and almost all reasoning-category answers are not contained in their passages, which conflicts with the instruction to answer only from them.

\subsection{QLoRA Fine-Tuning}
Both adapters use Unsloth and TRL on a single GPU with a 4-bit Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct base; LoRA rank 16, $\alpha=32$, dropout 0.05 on all attention and MLP projections; effective batch 16; learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$ with cosine decay and 5\% warm-up; 8-bit AdamW; maximum length 1024; three epochs. \textbf{Adapter~A}, used in the system-level study, was trained on the 702 examples of split~A with minority categories oversampled three times and loss over the full sequence; its final step-150 checkpoint was deployed. Training loss fell from 1.87 to 0.23 while validation loss was 0.577, 0.496 and 0.515 at steps 50, 100 and 150, so validation loss was lowest at step 100. \textbf{Adapters~B}, used in the generator-level study, are three retrained models (seeds 42--44) on split~B with loss on answer tokens only, no oversampling, and early stopping on validation loss (patience 3). Validation loss was lowest after only 11 steps (1.088--1.093) and training stopped at step 44 in all seeds; a run took about five minutes on a free-tier T4.

\section{Evaluation Design}\label{sec:eval}

\textbf{System-level study (Study~A).} We compare six systems: cloud only (S1), fine-tuned only (S2), base Qwen only (S5), RAG with the cloud model (S3), RAG with the fine-tuned model (S4) and RAG with the base model (S6). S2/S5 and S4/S6 differ only in the adapter. The question set comes from a bank of 32 gold questions, each compared with all 762 training and validation questions by normalised exact match, BGE cosine similarity $\ge0.82$ and an LLM check of whether memorising one would answer the other. Three exact matches and 12 paraphrases were excluded, leaving \textbf{17 screened questions} (11 conceptual, 6 numerical). All 102 answers were scored with RAGAS~\cite{es2024ragas} v0.4.3: faithfulness, answer relevancy and LLM context precision with reference. Faithfulness and context precision need retrieved context, so they are undefined for S1, S2 and S5. The judge is \texttt{openai/gpt-oss-120b} on Groq (temperature 0, low reasoning effort); an earlier judge was replaced because of a token-rate cap, and all reported scores come from this single judge. We report means with 95\% bootstrap intervals over questions (10{,}000 resamples) and paired differences over questions scored for both systems.

\textbf{Numerical benchmark.} Because RAGAS is a weak proxy for calculation, we generated 27 new problems (page replacement, disk scheduling, CPU scheduling; nine each) whose wording differs from the training templates, with ground truth from deterministic simulators. An answer is \emph{strict}-correct if its last number equals the truth and \emph{lenient}-correct if the truth appears among the numbers of its last 250 characters. Wilson intervals and an exact sign test are reported.

\textbf{Generator-level study (Study~B).} To isolate the generator from retrieval and from the judge, we gave each model the gold passage of 97 held-out items and compared its answer with the reference. Examples were re-split \emph{by passage} (Table~\ref{tab:audit}), with refusal examples grouped by question, so no passage is shared between splits and no training question is a near-duplicate (Jaccard $\ge0.9$) of a held-out one. Model selection used only validation data. The un-tuned base and the three adapters were scored under both system prompts with greedy decoding (at most 400 tokens). Metrics are exact match (EM), token-F1, ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge}, number recall (share of the reference's numbers present in the answer, numerical items only), refusal precision/recall/F1 with \texttt{OUT\_OF\_CONTEXT} as the positive class, false-refusal rate, and answer-token perplexity. Fine-tuned scores are averaged over seeds per item and compared with the base by a paired bootstrap over items (5{,}000 resamples; smallest attainable $p=2\times10^{-4}$).

\textbf{Protocol safeguards.} We cleared conversation memory before every request (earlier turns had leaked one system's answer into the next prompt), lowered the repetition penalty to 1.1 and removed the n-gram constraint (both apply to the prompt as well as the output and prevent quoting retrieved text), saved answers before judging, and corrected three wrong gold numerical references by simulation.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{System-Level Study}\label{sec:resA}
Table~\ref{tab:A} gives the six-system results. The cloud model with retrieval (S3) is the most faithful system. Paired over questions, S4 is lower than S3 on faithfulness by 0.268 (95\% CI $-0.445$ to $-0.106$); answer relevancy and context precision do not differ detectably. Against its own base (S4 $-$ S6), the fine-tuned model shows no detectable faithfulness gain ($-0.108$, CI $-0.285$ to $+0.050$) and lower answer relevancy ($-0.070$, CI $-0.108$ to $-0.033$); without retrieval the relevancy gap is $-0.130$ (S2 $-$ S5, CI $-0.186$ to $-0.080$). Adding retrieval did not change the base model's relevancy (S6 $-$ S5: $+0.002$, CI $-0.011$ to $+0.015$). Context precision is about 0.6 for the three RAG systems with no detectable differences, so retrieval, not the generator, is the common ceiling. One cloud refusal (\texttt{OUT\_OF\_CONTEXT} on the 8-bit address question) was scored 0 for relevancy; dropping it raises S3 relevancy from 0.872 to 0.927 and makes S4 $-$ S3 significantly negative ($-0.056$, CI $-0.097$ to $-0.020$), so the relevancy comparison depends on that rule.

\subsection{Generator-Level Study}\label{sec:resB}
Table~\ref{tab:B} and Fig.~\ref{fig:B} compare the base model with the three adapters on 97 held-out items. Under the training prompt, fine-tuning improves every overlap metric: token-F1 from 0.321 to $0.393\pm0.035$, ROUGE-L from 0.232 to $0.301\pm0.040$ and EM from 0.021 to $0.107\pm0.042$, with test perplexity falling from 2.83 to 2.53--2.55. Paired bootstrap confirms the gains (F1 $+0.072$, 95\% CI 0.039 to 0.104; ROUGE-L $+0.069$, CI 0.036 to 0.102; EM $+0.086$, CI 0.034 to 0.141; all $p<10^{-3}$). Answers become shorter (47 words versus 84; the mean reference has 67). By category, numerical problems improve from 0.268 to 0.376 F1 and recall from 0.416 to 0.466; the four reasoning categories have two test items each and are not interpreted.

\textbf{Prompt dependence.} Under the deployed prompt, which is the one used in the system-level study, the advantage disappears: F1 is 0.346 against 0.359 for the base model, within one seed s.d. (no paired test was run).

\textbf{Refusal.} Under the training prompt one of three adapters refused all five out-of-context items with no false refusals, whereas the other two never refused (hence $0.333\pm0.577$). The seed chosen on validation answered all five. No adapter produced a false refusal. Under the deployed prompt, which contains no refusal instruction, no model refused. Abstention is therefore learnable from this data but unstable.

\textbf{Qualitative errors.} The highest-F1 outputs are verbatim copies of definitions present in the passage, showing that the model learned to quote when it can. The lowest-F1 outputs are recall answers whose reference is not in the passage (fluent but off-point text) and single-word ``Yes''/``No'' replies to edge-case questions, one with the right polarity yet F1 of 0.02, which illustrates how token-F1 penalises terse correct answers.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What fine-tuning bought.} On gold passages and under the training prompt, the adapters give a reliable gain in overlap with reference answers and lower perplexity, mostly through shorter answers closer to the reference length and by copying from the passage; with real retrieved passages and the deployed prompt (Study~A), the same adapter does not beat its base on LLM-judged relevancy or faithfulness. The two studies agree once the prompt is accounted for: the adapter has learned the dataset's format under the prompt it was trained with. The dataset's curated, repeatedly reused passages differ from retrieved textbook chunks, which may explain why the gain does not transfer.

\textbf{Why refusal is unstable.} We did not isolate the cause, but the audit suggests four contributing factors: only 14 distinct refusal questions; early stopping after one third of an epoch; nearly half of the recall answers teaching the opposite behaviour (answer although the passage lacks the answer); and loss-based checkpoint selection, which cannot see a handful of short refusals. The all-or-nothing outcome across seeds points to a threshold effect.

\textbf{Retrieval and the judge.} Context precision of about 0.6 limits all RAG systems; for the opening question retrieval returned an anecdote about trains at a crossing rather than the formal definition. The judge belongs to the same family as the cloud generator, which may favour S1 and S3, so the cloud lead in faithfulness could be overstated; the S4--S6 comparison is unaffected because both are Qwen-based. Faithfulness also measures support by the retrieved context, not truth.

\textbf{Implications.} For educators who need privacy or offline use, the local system is functional but slow on CPU (median 56~s per answer versus 1.9~s) and does not match the cloud system's faithfulness or calculation accuracy. We recommend showing the retrieved passages beside each answer, serving the adapter only with the prompt it was trained on, and keeping instructor oversight until refusal is demonstrated on a larger set of unanswerable questions.

%==============================================================
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
The system-level study has 17 questions (6 numerical), so intervals are wide; the numerical benchmark has 27 problems and covers only the no-retrieval systems. Both studies rely on lexical or single-judge metrics, with no human evaluation. Qwen output was capped at 256 tokens; the adapter was trained on a 4-bit base but run in fp32 on CPU; and latency comes from one machine with interruptions. The generator-level test has 97 items, five refusal items and two items in each reasoning category, and its three seeds share one split. Passages are short curated entries, so Study~B shows generalisation to unseen passages, not unseen topics. One textbook, one model family and English only. We did not run ablations, so explanations of the refusal result are hypotheses.

%==============================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We built a RAG tutor for operating systems with interchangeable local and cloud generators and evaluated it under explicit leakage control. The cloud model with retrieval is the most faithful system. For the 1.5B model, LoRA fine-tuning on 762 examples yields a statistically reliable gain in answer overlap on held-out passages (token-F1 $+0.072$) but no gain in LLM-judged quality with retrieved passages, an unstable refusal behaviour, and a dependence on the system prompt. The accompanying audit explains these limits and gives concrete priorities: passages that actually contain each answer, far more diverse unanswerable questions, generation-based checkpoint selection, and prompt consistency between training and serving. OS is the first course in our plan: because the pipeline needs only a textbook and a question set, we intend to extend it to further courses built on their standard books, applying the same overlap screening and audit to each.
